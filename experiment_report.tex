\section{Research Questions}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1:}] How does quantization bit-width affect LLM performance on
        code clone detection tasks compared to full-precision baselines?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2:}] Which quantization methods (QTIP, AQLM, HIGGS-GPTQ,
        MatGPTQ, GGUF) best preserve code clone detection capability at
        equivalent bit-widths?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3:}] Is the performance impact of quantization consistent
        across programming languages and clone types?
  \item[\textbf{RQ4:}] What are the resource--performance trade-offs of
        quantized LLMs in code clone detection?
  \item[\textbf{RQ5:}] How does fine-tuning on code-clone-specific data affect
        the performance of quantized LLMs for code clone detection?
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Scope note.} We evaluate four quantization families: GGUF
$k$-quants (llama.cpp), AQLM, HIGGS-GPTQ, and QTIP. \emph{MatGPTQ} is listed in
RQ2 but was \textbf{not} evaluated in this study; RQ2 is answered for the four
families above and MatGPTQ is left as future work.

\subsection{Hardware and environment}
Experiments use two shared GPU servers, \texttt{tau} and \texttt{zeta}
(Table~\ref{tab:hardware}). \textbf{All results reported here were produced on
\texttt{tau}} (8$\times$NVIDIA H100 80\,GB HBM3, compute capability
\texttt{sm\_90}); \texttt{zeta} (8$\times$NVIDIA A100-SXM4 80\,GB,
\texttt{sm\_80}) is a second machine held in reserve for future runs and has not
yet been used. Each run is pinned to a single GPU via
\texttt{CUDA\_VISIBLE\_DEVICES}. Every backend uses an isolated Python~3.10
virtual environment with per-backend version pins; on \texttt{tau} the GGUF
(\texttt{llama-cpp-python}) and QTIP / HIGGS CUDA kernels are built from source
for \texttt{sm\_90} (these source-built artifacts are architecture-specific and
would need rebuilding on \texttt{zeta}'s \texttt{sm\_80}).

\subsection{Task and prompt}
Clone detection is framed as pairwise binary classification: given two code
snippets, the model answers \texttt{YES-SIMILAR}, \texttt{NO-NOT-SIMILAR}, or
\texttt{DONT-KNOW} as a JSON object (\{\texttt{answer}, \texttt{explanation}\}).
\texttt{YES-SIMILAR}$\rightarrow$CLONE and \texttt{NO-NOT-SIMILAR}$\rightarrow$
NON-CLONE; \texttt{DONT-KNOW} and unparseable answers are excluded from scoring.
The identical prompt is used for every model, quantization, and dataset.

GCJ test sets, giving a clean cross-dataset train/test split.

\subsection{Protocol and metrics}
Each configuration is run for \textbf{5 rounds}; the final label per pair is the
\textbf{majority vote} across rounds. We report Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1,
and the \textbf{Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)}. MCC is our primary
metric because it is robust on the balanced GCJ sets and heavily penalizes the
degenerate ``predict-everything-CLONE'' behavior some models exhibit
(MCC~$=0$). Comprehensive per-configuration results for each dataset are given
in Tables~\ref{tab:evaluation_metrics}--\ref{tab:evaluation_summary} (OCD),
and the analogous generated tables for GCJ-Java and GCJ cross-language
(Section~\ref{sec:fulltables}).

\section{RQ1: Bit-width vs.\ full precision}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------
Table~\ref{tab:rq1} sweeps the fully quantized Meta-Llama-3.1-8B across all
bit-widths and methods against its BF16 baseline, on all three datasets.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{RQ1 --- MCC vs.\ bit-width for Meta-Llama-3.1-8B (5-round majority
           vote). Higher bit-width monotonically improves MCC; the best 4-bit
           methods match or exceed the BF16 baseline.}
  \label{tab:rq1}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{llccc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Config} & \textbf{Bits} & \textbf{OCD} & \textbf{GCJ-Java} & \textbf{GCJ x-lang} \\
    \midrule
    BF16 (baseline)      & 16 & 0.473 & 0.458 & 0.325 \\
    \midrule
    AQLM                 & 2  & 0.151 & 0.176 & 0.103 \\
    GGUF Q2\_K           & 2  & 0.161 & 0.204 & 0.097 \\
    QTIP                 & 2  & 0.279 & 0.275 & 0.030 \\
    \midrule
    GGUF Q3\_K\_M        & 3  & 0.245 & 0.213 & 0.159 \\
    HIGGS-GPTQ           & 3  & 0.373 & 0.339 & 0.191 \\
    QTIP                 & 3  & 0.450 & 0.421 & 0.269 \\
    \midrule
    GGUF Q4\_K\_M        & 4  & 0.367 & 0.270 & 0.265 \\
    HIGGS-GPTQ           & 4  & 0.463 & 0.382 & 0.246 \\
    QTIP                 & 4  & \best{0.490} & \best{0.474} & \best{0.304} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent\textbf{Findings.} (i) MCC increases monotonically with bit-width
within every method (2b $<$ 3b $<$ 4b). (ii) Aggressive 2-bit quantization is
severely damaging: MCC collapses to $0.03$--$0.28$, far below BF16. (iii)
The impact is \emph{not} uniform across models: for the strong
\textbf{Qwen2.5-Coder-7B}, quantization is nearly lossless --- on OCD its BF16
MCC is $0.998$ and every GGUF quant (Q2/Q3/Q4) stays $\ge 0.998$; on GCJ-Java
Q2\_K ($0.825$) even \emph{exceeds} BF16 ($0.601$). Thus bit-width matters most
for weaker base models, while capable models tolerate low-bit quantization.

\section{RQ2: Which method best preserves capability at equal bit-width?}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------
Meta-Llama-3.1-8B is the only base model quantized by all four families, so it
isolates the effect of the \emph{method} at a fixed bit-width
(Table~\ref{tab:rq2}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{RQ2 --- method comparison at equal bit-width (Meta-Llama-3.1-8B,
           MCC). \best{Bold} = best method at that bit-width. QTIP dominates at
           every bit-width. MatGPTQ was not evaluated.}
  \label{tab:rq2}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    & \multicolumn{3}{c}{\textbf{MCC (best method in bold)}}\\
    \cmidrule(lr){2-4}
    \textbf{Bit-width / method} & \textbf{OCD} & \textbf{GCJ-Java} & \textbf{GCJ x-lang} \\
    \midrule
    \textit{2-bit} \\
    \quad AQLM         & 0.151 & 0.176 & 0.103 \\
    \quad GGUF Q2\_K   & 0.161 & 0.204 & \best{0.097} \\
    \quad QTIP         & \best{0.279} & \best{0.275} & 0.030 \\
    \midrule
    \textit{3-bit} \\
    \quad GGUF Q3\_K\_M & 0.245 & 0.213 & 0.159 \\
    \quad HIGGS-GPTQ    & 0.373 & 0.339 & 0.191 \\
    \quad QTIP          & \best{0.450} & \best{0.421} & \best{0.269} \\
    \midrule
    \textit{4-bit} \\
    \quad GGUF Q4\_K\_M & 0.367 & 0.270 & 0.265 \\
    \quad HIGGS-GPTQ    & 0.463 & 0.382 & 0.246 \\
    \quad QTIP          & \best{0.490} & \best{0.474} & \best{0.304} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent\textbf{Findings.} \textbf{QTIP} (trellis-coded quantization) is the
strongest preservation method: it wins at 3-bit and 4-bit on all three datasets
and at 2-bit on the two Java sets. QTIP-4bit even surpasses the BF16 baseline on
OCD and GCJ-Java (Table~\ref{tab:rq1}). \textbf{HIGGS-GPTQ} is a consistent
second at 3--4 bit. \textbf{GGUF} $k$-quants and \textbf{AQLM} are the weakest at
matched bit-width (AQLM-2bit and GGUF-Q2\_K are near-degenerate). The lone 2-bit
exception is the cross-language set, where all three 2-bit methods are close to
random. MatGPTQ is not evaluated and remains future work.

\section{RQ3: Consistency across languages and clone types}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------
Table~\ref{tab:rq3} compares each model's best-per-family configuration across
the three datasets, which vary in language (Java vs.\ multi-language) and clone
type (syntactic OCD vs.\ semantic GCJ).

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{RQ3 --- MCC across datasets (representative configuration per model;
           GGUF Q4\_K\_M unless noted). ``$\Delta$ x-lang'' is GCJ
           cross-language minus GCJ-Java.}
  \label{tab:rq3}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lcccc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Model (config)} & \textbf{OCD} & \textbf{GCJ-Java} & \textbf{GCJ x-lang} & \textbf{$\Delta$ x-lang} \\
    \midrule
    Qwen2.5-Coder-7B (Q4\_K\_M)       & 0.998 & 0.682 & 0.734 & $+0.052$ \\
    Llama-4-Scout-17B (Q4\_K\_M)      & 0.908 & 0.923 & 0.933 & $+0.010$ \\
    DeepSeek-V2-Lite (Q4\_K\_M)       & 0.733 & 0.816 & 0.747 & $-0.069$ \\
    Meta-Llama-3.1-8B (Q4\_K\_M)      & 0.367 & 0.270 & 0.265 & $-0.005$ \\
    CodeLlama-7b (Q4\_K\_M)           & 0.048 & 0.051 & 0.000 & $-0.051$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent\textbf{Findings.} The impact of quantization is \emph{not} uniform;
it is governed mainly by base-model capability, and this holds across languages
and clone types. (i) \textbf{Clone type matters more than language}: syntactic
OCD is nearly saturated for the strongest model (Qwen $0.998$) but hard for weak
ones, whereas the semantic GCJ sets are uniformly harder. (ii)
\textbf{Cross-language is roughly as hard as monolingual Java for strong
models} (Scout, Qwen even improve slightly), but weak models degrade further
cross-language (CodeLlama $0.051\!\rightarrow\!0.000$). (iii) The degenerate
failure mode (predicting all-CLONE, MCC~$=0$) is consistent for CodeLlama across
all datasets and quantizations. Overall ranking is stable across datasets:
Scout/Qwen $>$ DeepSeek $>$ Llama-3.1 $>$ CodeLlama.

\section{RQ4: Resource--performance trade-offs}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------
Quantization reduces the weight footprint by roughly $4\times$ (4-bit) to
$8\times$ (2-bit) relative to BF16. Table~\ref{tab:rq4} places representative
configurations on the resource ($\approx$ effective bits $\times$ params) vs.\
performance (GCJ-Java MCC) plane.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{RQ4 --- resource vs.\ performance (GCJ-Java, 5-round majority vote).
           Approx.\ weight memory assumes the nominal bit-width; MoE rows use
           total parameters.}
  \label{tab:rq4}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{llrrc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Config} & \textbf{Bits} & \textbf{$\approx$Wt.\ mem.} & \textbf{MCC} \\
    \midrule
    Qwen2.5-Coder-7B   & BF16       & 16 & $\sim$14\,GB & 0.601 \\
    Qwen2.5-Coder-7B   & Q2\_K      &  2 & $\sim$2.2\,GB & \best{0.825} \\
    Qwen2.5-Coder-7B   & Q4\_K\_M   &  4 & $\sim$4.4\,GB & 0.682 \\
    \midrule
    Llama-4-Scout-17B  & Q4\_K\_M   &  4 & $\sim$55\,GB & 0.923 \\
    DeepSeek-V2-Lite   & Q4\_K\_M   &  4 & $\sim$8\,GB  & 0.816 \\
    \midrule
    Meta-Llama-3.1-8B  & BF16       & 16 & $\sim$16\,GB & 0.458 \\
    Meta-Llama-3.1-8B  & QTIP-4b    &  4 & $\sim$4.5\,GB & 0.474 \\
    Meta-Llama-3.1-8B  & QTIP-2b    &  2 & $\sim$2.5\,GB & 0.275 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent\textbf{Findings.} (i) A \textbf{strong small model at low precision
dominates the trade-off}: Qwen2.5-Coder-7B at Q2\_K ($\sim$2\,GB) reaches MCC
$0.825$ on GCJ-Java, beating a $\sim$55\,GB Q4 model on some axes and vastly
outperforming BF16 Llama-3.1-8B ($0.458$) at $\sim$1/7 the memory. (ii) For a
capable base, 4-bit is effectively ``free'' (QTIP-4b $\ge$ BF16), so 4-bit is
the recommended default operating point. (iii) 2-bit only pays off for models
robust to it (Qwen); for weak bases it destroys accuracy and is not worth the
extra compression. Model \emph{choice} therefore buys far more than spending
resources on higher precision of a weak model.

\section{RQ5: Clone-specific fine-tuning of quantized models}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------
We fine-tune a LoRA adapter with \textbf{QLoRA} (4-bit NF4 frozen base, rank-16
adapter, $\sim$40M trainable params, 3 epochs) on the AIZU384F clone pairs, then
apply the adapter \emph{on top of the same Q4\_K\_M GGUF} at inference (via
llama.cpp \texttt{lora\_path}). The base bytes are identical to the evaluated
Q4\_K\_M baseline, so the delta isolates the fine-tuning effect at fixed
quantization. Training (AIZU) and testing (GCJ) use disjoint sources.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{RQ5 --- QLoRA fine-tuning on the Q4\_K\_M base (MCC,
           baseline~$\rightarrow$~fine-tuned). Adapter trained on AIZU384F;
           the test sets (GCJ) share no problems with training.}
  \label{tab:rq5}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lcccc}
    \toprule
    & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{GCJ-Java MCC}} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{GCJ x-lang MCC}}\\
    \cmidrule(lr){2-3}\cmidrule(lr){4-5}
    \textbf{Model (Q4\_K\_M base)} & base & +QLoRA & base & +QLoRA \\
    \midrule
    Qwen2.5-Coder-7B  & 0.682 & \best{0.955} & 0.734 & \best{0.912} \\
    Meta-Llama-3.1-8B & 0.270 & \best{0.889} & 0.265 & \best{0.876} \\
    CodeLlama-7b      & 0.051 & \best{0.769} & 0.000 & \best{0.754} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{RQ5 (ablation) --- effect of training-set composition for Qwen2.5-Coder-7B
           (MCC). Training on monolingual (AIZU384F) vs.\ cross-language
           (AIZU324CLF) pairs is statistically indistinguishable on both tests.}
  \label{tab:rq5-ablation}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Adapter (training data)} & \textbf{GCJ-Java} & \textbf{GCJ x-lang} \\
    \midrule
    none (Q4\_K\_M baseline)       & 0.682 & 0.734 \\
    AIZU384F (monolingual)         & 0.955 & 0.912 \\
    AIZU324CLF (cross-language)    & 0.955 & 0.917 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent\textbf{Findings.} (i) Fine-tuning yields \textbf{large gains at fixed
quantization}: $+0.27$ to $+0.72$ MCC. (ii) It \textbf{rescues degenerate
quantized models}: CodeLlama-7b Q4\_K\_M goes from MCC $0.000$ (all-CLONE) to
$0.754$ cross-language. (iii) The \textbf{absolute lift is largest for the
weakest base} (CodeLlama $+0.72$, Llama-3.1 $+0.62$, Qwen $+0.27$), but the
final ranking still tracks base capability (Qwen $\sim$0.93 $>$ Llama-3.1
$\sim$0.88 $>$ CodeLlama $\sim$0.76): fine-tuning narrows, but does not erase,
the base-model gap. (iv) \textbf{Training-set composition does not matter}
(Table~\ref{tab:rq5-ablation}): a monolingual-trained adapter transfers to
cross-language clone detection as well as a cross-language-trained one, i.e.\
QLoRA learns a general functional-similarity skill. All fine-tuned runs produced
0 unparseable/DONT-KNOW answers, so the gains are genuine capability, not output
formatting.
